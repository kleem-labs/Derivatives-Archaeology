# Readability Audit — Loop 1

**Question used:** could a reader first picture the idea with ordinary objects, then state its exact finance meaning, without needing a later chapter to decode a word?

## What was checked across Chapters 000–049

Every chapter now has these entry gates, in this order:

1. **Vocabulary key** — points to prior ideas and the new terms in the Concept Atlas.
2. **A small picture** — an everyday story with no formula required.
3. **In finance language** — the exact term and its job.
4. **Everyday explanation** — a short bridge into the chapter’s full argument.

The audit treats these as separate jobs. The picture gives intuition. The finance sentence gives precision. Neither is allowed to replace the other.

## How a reader should give feedback for the next loop

Paste the exact paragraph and say one of these:

- `I do not know this word.`
- `I see the formula but not the story.`
- `I see the story but not why the formula follows.`
- `I do not know what decision this changes.`
- `This example changes too many things at once.`

That feedback is a bug report against the book, not a failure by the reader. The repair order is always: define the object, show a smaller example, reconnect the formula, then state the decision consequence.

## Limits of the phrase “five years old”

A five-year-old can understand every **picture** in the entry layer: promises, coupons, ladders, boxes, scores, and paths. A reader learning the mathematics will still need to practice the finance layer—especially probability, calculus, and numerical methods. The book’s promise is not that advanced mathematics becomes childish; it is that no advanced term appears without an earlier picture, a definition, and a stated reason for existing.
